The big split

Isometric or isotonic?

Every muscle contraction falls into one of two categories.

IsometricIsotonic
Tension develops without the muscle changing length.The muscle shortens or lengthens while generating force.

Isometric

Tension without movement

Muscle tension develops without changing its length. This is essential for maintaining posture and stabilizing joints during activity.

Example: holding a plank or a wall sit.

Isotonic · type 1

Concentric: the muscle shortens

Isotonic contractions involve the muscle shortening or lengthening while generating force. In a concentric contraction, the muscle shortens while generating force, allowing movement — like lifting a weight.

Unit 2.5 Muscular System · Day 6 · Mon, Oct 26slide 6 of 16
Isotonic · type 2

Eccentric: the muscle lengthens under tension

The muscle lengthens while still under tension. This happens when lowering weights, where the fibers gradually elongate to control the descent and resist gravity.

Example: the biceps lowering a dumbbell slowly.

Back to the sliding filament theory

Cross-bridges form every time

Actin and myosin filaments slide past each other to shorten the sarcomere. But even during an isometric contraction, where there is no net shortening, cross-bridges still form — they just create tension instead of movement.

Summarize

What are the types of muscle contraction, and what do they have in common?

Contractions are isometric or isotonic. In an isometric contraction the muscle makes tension but stays the same length. Isotonic contractions change length: concentric shortens the muscle, and eccentric lengthens it under tension. All of them use the same cross-bridge process.
